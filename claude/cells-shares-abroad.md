# The households' shares sold abroad, in the balance of payments

The exchange rate reads the balance of payments: the current account and
the financial account over a year, against the city's GDP. A household that
sells a bond to the world has always shown on the financial account as
money coming in. A household that sold a share to the world did not: the
exchange kept the month's sum, but the money audit never declared it, so
the balance of payments, and the rate, never saw those dollars arrive.

LF1b measured it before changing anything. On main's default playtest the
households sold shares abroad in 968 of 2,404 months. Over the whole run it
is a small part of the capital flows, but not where it happens: in months
401-800 those sales were a fifth of all the capital coming in, and larger
than the financial account's whole net, which they turn from a deficit to a
small surplus. After month 1200 the hot money and the bonds dwarf them.

The audit now declares each sale abroad as a financial inflow into the
households' savings and each purchase from the world as an outflow out of
them, with the signs the households' bonds have always had. Each is a pair
the cash identity cancels, so the audit's residual cannot catch a missing
one; ShortSellingCheck causes both trades inside a real month and reads the
lines themselves.

This changes how the game plays. Where households sell shares abroad
heavily the currency stays stronger: in the default playtest the rate was
53% lower through months 401-800, the city grew slowly where main's boomed
on a falling currency, then overtook it, ending with 1.39 million people
against 0.47 million. A city whose households trade little abroad plays as
before. The ensemble's verdicts across 80 runs, the figures and the checks
are in [the LF1b notes](../docs/queue/LF1b-notes.md).
