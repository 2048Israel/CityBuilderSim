Mockup 4 — Equipment on a sector's page

Equipment is an investment, so it lives on the Investors page (0.7.30's sector frame: Operations, Income, Balance sheet, Cash & debt, Investors). A refit is ranked against new buildings by what it earns on its cost (study §2.2); each piece of equipment gets a card saying what it does, where it comes from, what the next one adds in the spread's own terms, and, per kind of building, how many are fitted and why the rest are not: fitted, next, not worth it yet (it fails over its life) or won't fit (the building has too few years left, or needs something first).

  • The scanner (study §4, B5): a store's payroll falls 5.35% for the same sales [Basker 2012]. A lane cost US$13,750 in 1978 (US$110k for 8 lanes) and D$ 68k here (est.: × 4.92, the US CPI to 2025). A Small Grocery Store's 27 posts cost D$ 107.9k a month (`buildings.json`, `PayTier`), so it saves D$ 5.77k on four lanes and pays back in 3.9 years; a Convenience Store D$ 1.09k on one lane, 5.2 years; a Filling Station D$ 0.61k, and over a scanner's 10-year life (est.) at the investors' 7.1% that is D$ 52k of the D$ 68k.
  • The caster (E9): +10% steel from the same melt, 10–15% less labour, about 1 MBtu a ton less fuel, US$40–80 per tonne of yearly capacity in 1978 [OTA 1979]. An Electric Arc Furnace (1,200 t a month) gets D$ 78.0k of steel (120 t at D$ 650, the materials study's landed price; the city imports steel), D$ 19.4k of posts (3 no diploma, 2 diploma), D$ 19.5k of fuel (32,500 L at D$ 0.60), less a quarter of it as the licence fee until 1982: D$ 87.7k a month for D$ 4.25M (14,400 t a year × US$60 × 4.92). Furnaces 1 and 2 have four years left; over four years it earns back D$ 3.7M.
  • The money gate is the game's own (1.25 × the interest at 7.1%: 0.74% of the cost a month). The Filling Station's scanner passes it (0.90%) and still fails over its life, which is Jerus's long-run rule.
