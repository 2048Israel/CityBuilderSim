Mockup 4 — wood: Forestry and Wood Products

Forestry cuts the city's own forest. Forest is terrain, not a field: every square kilometre of it stands at 13,700 m³ (Canada's real average is 137 m³ a hectare, the same figure), and a cut hectare grows back by the game's existing rule. A harvest is half sawlogs and half pulpwood. Wood Products owns the sawmills, the panel mill, the pulp mill, the paper mill and the pellet mill, and builds whichever earns the most on its cost. A sawmill turns half a log into lumber and four tenths into chips and sawdust, which the pulp mill takes.

Same city and month. Ribbons are to scale in cubic metres of wood; pulp is drawn at the 4.5 m³ of wood a tonne of it takes. The builders are drawn as they will be after the construction upgrade, when buildings are made of lumber and panels; until then that lumber goes abroad.

Forestry — the forest. Two natural woods worked by machine crews, and twelve plantations Forestry planted on bare ground decades ago, now cut by hand crews.
  • The cut is bigger than the regrowth, and the page says where that ends. With the game's rule a cut hectare grows back by 1/240 of what was taken each month, so the more is cut, the faster the forest grows back. At 75,000 m³ a month from the natural woods it settles where regrowth equals the cut: 29% standing, in about 45 years. There is no limit on the cut (report Q14); the page shows where it ends instead.
  • A plantation is the lever, and it is Forestry's. Planted pine grows 15–40 m³ a hectare a year against a natural forest's 1–6 (report §5.1); twelve plantations on 120 km² add 20,000 m³ a month. A plantation waits 25 years for its first cut, so it never passes the interest test. Forestry plants one anyway, as a cost of cutting, whenever its cut exceeds regrowth and the city has 1,000 ha of bare dry land (report Q15). Here it has 412 ha, so the line points at the land office.
Wood Products — the mills. Two sawmills feed the pulp mill their chips and sawdust; the panel mill takes pulpwood; the pulp mill buys the rest of its wood abroad.
  • The by-product is a feed. A sawmill sells 41% of every log as chips and sawdust. Pulp pays D$ 92 for a cubic metre of them; a pellet mill would pay D$ 54. So while the pulp mill is short, the rule builds no pellet mill. A town with a sawmill and no pulp mill builds one instead.
  • The paper mill is on the list now (report Q16), and not built here. On the city's own pulp at D$ 600, paper sold abroad at D$ 880 only covers its costs (0.0% a month); where it replaces imported paper at D$ 1,050 it earns 0.6%. So it arrives where the city buys paper, and nothing here buys paper yet: the shops' packaging is the natural first buyer (report §5.6).
