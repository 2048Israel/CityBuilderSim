Mockup 1 — iron: Iron Mining and Iron & Steel

The iron industry redone as two sectors. Iron Mining lifts ore from the city's fields; a field is either hematite (shipped as it is dug, 62% iron) or magnetite (30% iron, concentrated three tonnes to one at the mine). Iron & Steel (today's Heavy Industry) owns every building that turns ore, coal, limestone and scrap into iron and steel, and builds whichever earns the most on its cost at the city's own prices (report §1.2).

Both windows are the same big city in the same month as the oil mockups (June 2183, 2.61M people). Ribbons are to scale in tonnes of what flows, so a unit's box is as tall as the larger of what goes in and what comes out; the difference is what the process takes away (water and rock at the mine, oxygen and slag at the furnace). Every flow balances; figures are worked in the report's model (§2, §8).

Iron Mining — the mines. Four fields: two worked hard, one nearly empty, one just bought. Each field is drawn as a vessel filled to what is left; the ribbon beside it is what it gave up this month.
  • The grade is the mechanism, not a modifier. A hematite field is dug and shipped; a magnetite field needs a concentrator, which is why its mine costs more, uses 52 MW and 7,900 units of water, and why its box is three times as tall as what comes out of it.
  • The mine types fit the free sites. A Large Open-Pit Mine stands on 20 sites of one field; West 9 has 4, so the investors put Iron Mines there. North 2 runs out in 22 years and its one mine then retires by the existing worked-out rule.
  • Ore that nobody here wants still sells, at the export price, so a mine is worth building before any furnace exists (the rule from steel-and-heavy-industry.md, kept).
Iron & Steel — the steelworks. Two routes side by side: the blast furnace and oxygen steel shop on the city's own ore, coal and limestone; the electric arc furnaces on scrap. The products pictogram is filled by what the works made this month.
  • One sector, many buildings, one rule: whatever earns most. Coke ovens, blast furnaces, the oxygen shop, arc furnaces, scrap yards and the pelletising plant are all Iron & Steel's. Each month it values every good at the city's own price and builds the one that earns the most on its cost, if that clears the interest test (report §1.2). Nothing else holds it back: with freight in the price bands, ore bought abroad to make steel for abroad does not pay on its own.
  • The investors' line is the rule speaking. Concentrate goes abroad at D$ 85 while pellets fetch D$ 125, so a second pelletising plant earns 1.8% a month. A third coke oven would replace the 11,750 t of imported coke and sell the rest abroad: 1.2%. The city is short of 18,900 t of steel, but an arc furnace on imported scrap earns only 0.2%.
  • Steel is priced as the world prices it: D$ 650 landed, D$ 470 if exported (report Q6). At those prices the works sells its steel at home, Manufacturing's fabricators grew to use it, and the last 18,900 t comes in by sea. Fabricated steel and machinery moved down with it, so their margins over steel are what they were.
  • Scrap is a city product now. Old cars, vans and demolished buildings arise as old steel; a Scrap Yard buys it and sells scrap. Iron ore no longer stands in for scrap (report Q4).
