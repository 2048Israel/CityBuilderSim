Mockup 1 — the refinery pictogram (Jerus's idea)

Refining's Operations page. Crude comes in on the left. The column is filled with what the crude gives (its cuts, in boiling order: gas at the top, residue at the bottom). Each cut either goes straight to a product or through a conversion unit. The refinery pictogram in the middle is filled in coloured bands by how much of each product it made this month. The buyers are on the right; imported product joins them hatched, and what nobody here buys goes abroad. Every ribbon is to scale.

Figures are worked from the research report (§1–§2): real straight-run yields, the game unit yields marked (est.) there, and prices as ratios to crude. The two cities are illustrative; within each, every flow balances (crude in = products + refinery fuel; made + imported = used here + exported).

State A — a small town with one simple refinery. 41,300 people, 22 land wells, one 2,000 b/d crude unit with a small reformer (a "hydroskimming" plant). It refines only its own crude.
  • A simple plant sends 33% of what it makes abroad as fuel oil, at a discount. Its petrol covers 95% of the town's cars; the last 0.10M L is imported.
  • It pays only because the crude is the town's own (crude at its export price, D$0.399/L). On imported crude (D$0.469/L) the same plant loses D$0.05M a month before wages. That is the real economics of small simple refineries, and it is what pushes the investors toward conversion units.
State B — a big city with several conversion units. 2.61M people, two offshore platforms, 160 land wells and imported crude; a 100,000 b/d crude unit at 80%, a reformer, a cracking unit, a lube plant and an asphalt unit, with an alkylation unit on site.
  • The city is short of petrol (36.8M L imported) and long of gas (29.0M L sold abroad at D$0.18/L). Alkylation turns gas into petrol, so it has the widest spread (+D$0.27 per litre of feed): the investors' line says it is being built.
  • Fuel oil is the big surplus (47.5M L abroad). A coker would turn residue into diesel, but at today's prices it loses D$0.03 a litre, so none is built. The rule in report §2.3 decides this; nothing is scripted.
  • Buyers that don't exist in the game yet (Airport, Ships, Roads for bitumen, Industry for lubricants and gas) are drawn as they would be. Until they exist, their share goes abroad. See the report's phasing (§2.2).
