# Gathered: the mental hospital and chlorpromazine

*Gathered 2026-11-17 for MED1 by a Sonnet agent; marks: page = seen on the opened page, snippet = search summary only, memory = not verified.*

How the marks were earned. Statistics Canada tables were read as the CSV files behind the Historical Statistics of Canada (through a summarising fetch tool, so key figures were cross-checked against a second table). The DBS "Mental Health Statistics" volumes and Year Book pages were downloaded as PDFs and read from their OCR text; where the OCR scrambled row labels I matched rows by sequence and checked them against rate times census population ("OCR"). "Derived" or "computed" means my arithmetic from the page figures named. Nothing is copied wording.

## 1. Patients and beds, 1900 to today

**Nothing for 1900-1930 was found on a page.** The 1931 census is described as the first special census of mental institutions by the Dominion Bureau of Statistics (snippet, MH-5), so no comparable national series earlier exists; provincial inspectors' annual reports are the lead (not opened). The game's series can start at 1931 (3.05 per 1,000) or use a back-cast; I invented none.

Four counts of "the patients" exist and must not be mixed: A = every mental institution at 31 December (Year Book, 1932-56); B = public mental hospitals only, patients on the books (DBS, 1955-63); C = all mental institutions incl. units and hospitals for the mentally deficient, patients on the books (DBS 1964); D = average daily census, patient-days divided by 365 (Historical Statistics).

**US check.** TAC lists 558,922 state-hospital beds in 1955, 337.0 per 100,000, falling to 37,679 staffed beds, 11.7 per 100,000, in 2016 (page, MH-14); it labels 1955 as beds, not as residents. The figure usually quoted for resident patients is 558,239 (page, an excerpt on a PBS page quoting the psychiatrist Torrey, population about 164 million so about 340 per 100,000; MH-15). The Science History Institute profile says residents fell from about 559,000 in 1955 to about 452,000 ten years later (page, MH-9). So the "558,922" you gave is TAC's bed figure, not confirmed as a resident count; use "about 559,000". Canada's 1955 rate was 359 per 100,000 in public mental hospitals, 390 with units and 447 with the mentally-deficient hospitals (derived), so Canada was as institutionalised as the US, not less.

## 2. Chlorpromazine

**Timeline.**

**FDA date: unconfirmed.** Secondary sources say 1954 (one says approved by May 1954; snippet); 26 March 1954 is in my memory, no page found (memory). Drugs@FDA's record for NDA 009149 shows 20 November 1957 as the original approval (page, MH-10), probably a database artefact for an old drug. **The Canadian sale date was not found**; only that 1953 use ran under the maker's evaluation permit (snippet). Verdun Protestant Hospital held nearly 1,500 inpatients in 1942 and 908 beds in 1981; renamed Douglas Hospital in 1965 (page, MH-8).

**Measured effect.**

- Cochrane review of chlorpromazine against placebo (2007; 50 studies): the share not improved was 20 per cent lower on the drug (risk ratio 0.80, 13 trials, 1,121 people, number needed to treat 6); relapse risk with the drug was 0.57 of placebo's in longer trials (3 trials, 512 people, number needed to treat 4), 0.49 in medium (809 people); sedation 2.6 times as likely, parkinsonism 2.0, weight gain 4.9; people on the drug left trials early less often (0.65) (page, MH-12).
- NIMH 1964: the Cochrane characteristics table lists this study with 463 people, arms of 112 (chlorpromazine), 115 (fluphenazine), 111 (thioridazine) and 125 (placebo), half first-episode (snippet). **The improved-versus-placebo percentages were not found on a page.** From memory only: about three in four drug patients much or very much improved against about one in four on placebo (memory, unverified).
- Canada's own numbers (public mental hospitals, DBS, page OCR, MH-3): 1955 to 1963 admissions 14,960 to 27,288 (plus 82 per cent), discharges 10,770 to 24,747 (plus 130 per cent), deaths 3,016 to 3,808, patient-days per 100 people 121 to 97. Discharges more than doubled while head count rose to 1960 and then fell 4 per cent in three years (59,308 to 56,975). Average count in hospital over the whole period fell about 6 per cent, 49,537 (1955) to 46,498 (1963) (page, MH-16).
- **Who left?** Richman (1966): patients already long-stay in 1955 left at the same rate in 1960-63 as in 1955-59, no new hard core of long-stay patients had formed by 1963, and the share of new admissions that stayed continuously hospitalised kept falling, notably for old people and for psychotics (page, MH-16). The DBS says the same in its own words: the fall in the head count came from shorter stays, not from emptied wards (page, MH-3, footnote 3).
- Share of schizophrenia patients who could leave: no Canadian page gave it. Derived from DBS: for schizophrenia, 5,054 left in 1955 against 25,087 on the books (page, OCR), about one in five a year before the policy changes. New York State (Brill and Patton): the census fell by about 500 in the first drug year after rising 2,500 the year before, and releases rose from 14,362 (1955) to 19,334 (1958) (snippet).

**Drug or policy?** Gronfein (1985) found no break in discharge rates at the date the drugs arrived, and argued the drugs worked through policy (snippet, MH-19). Policy dates: US Medicare and Medicaid 1965, the push that came ten years after the drug (page, MH-15). Canada: the Hospital Insurance and Diagnostic Services Act, 1957 (all provinces by 1961) paid federal money for general hospital care but not for mental hospitals or sanatoria (snippet, secondary sources; the statute was not read); my inference is that a psychiatric bed in a general hospital then cost a province less. The Hall Commission (1961-64) estimated that the mental-hospital ratio could fall from 3.0 to 1.5 per 1,000 between 1961 and 1971 (page, MH-16). Sealy and Whitehead (2004, abstract): psychiatric hospital beds closed fast in the 1970s and 1980s, days of care in general-hospital psychiatric units rose at the same time, total inpatient days did not begin to fall until the 1990s, and provinces differed much in timing (page, MH-6). So in Canada the **drug shortened stays from about 1955; beds fell mainly after 1965 through policy and money**; the head count fell 1960-63, the beds after 1965.

## 4. The building

**Size then.** 1955: 52 public mental hospitals held 56,416 patients, about 1,085 each; 1963: 45 hospitals, 56,975, about 1,270 (derived; hospital counts OCR, MH-3). Counting every institution the 1956 Year Book has 104 mental institutions (including psychiatric units) holding 65,107. By province, 31 December 1956 (page, OCR, MH-5): Ontario 21,172 patients (capacity 18,409), Quebec 18,663, BC 6,385, Saskatchewan 4,577, Alberta 4,466, Manitoba 3,681, Nova Scotia 2,902, New Brunswick 1,828, Newfoundland 952, PEI 481. In 1934 normal capacity was 12,666 in Ontario and 9,850 in Quebec (page, MH-5). Single hospitals (snippets, MH-23): Riverview (Essondale), BC, about 4,300 to 4,700 patients in the early to mid 1950s (sources differ: 4,630 in 1951, 4,726 in 1955, 4,306 in 1956); Whitby, Ontario, capacity 1,542 in 1927 and about 1,650 patients at its peak; Brockville over 1,000 soon after the war. Verdun Protestant held nearly 1,500 in 1942 (page, MH-8). 1,000 to 5,000 beds is the right span, with a mean near 1,100.

**Size now.** In 1975, 114 public mental hospitals averaged 172 patients a day, down from 191 in 1974 (page, MH-4). In 2019-20 the largest specialty mental health hospitals by staffed mental health beds were CAMH Toronto 497, Ontario Shores 345, Homewood 303, Waypoint 301, Alberta Hospital Edmonton 295, Saskatchewan Hospital 284, the Centennial Centre (Ponoka) 282, Royal Ottawa 260 (page, MH-17); the 75 hospitals whose type includes the specialty-mental-health code (a few are also general hospitals) hold 5,635 mental health beds, about 75 each, and the other 111 hospitals with any mental health beds hold 6,702 (derived).

## 5. The people who need it

**Prevalence.** Diagnosed schizophrenia: 1 in 100 people aged 10 and over in 2016-17, 56 per cent men; the count rose about 3 per cent a year, 2002-2016; new cases fell; 147,500 people used health services for it (page, MH-20; counts people in contact with care). Serious mental illness has no single Canadian figure; for the wider group, 18 per cent of people 15 and over met criteria for a mood, anxiety or substance-use disorder in the past year in 2022, and bipolar disorder 2.1 per cent (1.5 per cent in 2012) (page, MH-21). A "serious mental illness" rate for the game must be chosen; 1 per cent for psychosis plus about 2 per cent for bipolar is the supportable sum (derived, overlaps unknown).

**Work.** No authoritative Canadian employment rate for schizophrenia was found on a page. Candidates, all weak: about 15 per cent (a newspaper, unsourced; snippet); 10 to 30 per cent for severe mental disorders (a 2025 slide deck citing a 2013 report; snippet); 13 to 55 per cent in first-episode psychosis programmes in Ontario (a 2012 review; snippet). In At Home/Chez Soi, 93 per cent of the homeless people with serious mental illness were unemployed at entry, though more than 66 per cent had worked steadily in the past (page, MH-25).

**Excess death.** Ontario 1993-2012 (Gatov and others, CMAJ 2017, record read): all-cause mortality about 3.1 times higher with schizophrenia (adjusted rate ratio 3.12), the gap in age-standardised death rates narrowed from 16.15 to 10.49 per 1,000, people with schizophrenia died on average about 8 years younger; deaths 31,349 against 1,589,902 (page, MH-22 record; the abstract gives no years-of-life-lost number). The Public Health Agency says 2.8 times (page, MH-20). Standardised mortality ratio and years of life lost in Canada: not found as numbers.

**Homeless.** At Home/Chez Soi (2009-13): 2,148 people with serious mental illness, 1,158 got Housing First, 990 usual care; 34 per cent had a psychotic disorder, 71 per cent a non-psychotic one, 67 per cent substance problems; in the last six months 62 per cent of the Housing First group were housed all the time against 31 per cent of usual care; the cost of the intervention was $22,257 per person a year (page, MH-25). The 2020-22 point-in-time counts (about 22,000 respondents to the question): 47 per cent reported both mental health and substance-use issues, 13 per cent mental health only, 14 per cent substance use only, 27 per cent neither, self-reported (snippet, MH-26). Surveys of homeless people in western countries: psychotic illness from 2.8 to 42.3 per cent across 29 surveys, 5,684 people, pooled about 12.7 per cent (range page, pooled value memory, MH-27, CC BY).

**Did closing beds cause homelessness?** For: ecological studies find that places with fewer psychiatric beds have more homeless and imprisoned mentally ill people, and Canada cut beds steeply (a 2024 review is quoted as saying minus 70.6 per cent from 1965 to 1981; snippet, MH-28; that looks too steep against the 30 per cent fall to 1975 in MH-1, unverified). Against: a systematic review of 23 studies of people discharged after stays of a year or more found most studies identified no homelessness, imprisonment or suicide afterwards (Winkler and others 2016, via the Salisbury and Thornicroft commentary, page, MH-28); a 2024 review says the causal link was questioned, probably confounded by other social change, and that homelessness is mainly a housing-supply problem (snippet). No Canadian study that tests it directly was found. Net: weak evidence for a large direct effect, the housing market the better-supported cause.

**Jails.** Correctional Service Canada's study of 1,110 men entering federal custody (2012-14, published 2015): over 70 per cent met criteria for at least one mental disorder, about 40 per cent when substance use and antisocial personality disorder are left out, 12.4 per cent had a current major mental illness (bipolar, major depression or psychotic disorder), 3.3 per cent a psychotic disorder (page, MH-29). The federal prison population count was not retrieved, so the number of people is missing (memory: roughly 14,000, unverified).

## 6. Notes

- **Which series for the game.** Per-1,000 rates survive the changing definitions; counts do not. Spine: patients per 1,000 (A: 3.05 in 1931, 3.64 in 1936, 3.93 in 1941, 4.01 in 1946, 3.96 in 1951, 4.11 in 1954, 4.05 in 1956), rated beds per 1,000 (about 3.5 in 1955, 3.6 in 1961-64, 2.8 in 1970, 2.0 to 2.4 in 1975), then 0.33 (2019-20, CIHI, not cleared). 1900-1930 and 1976-2018 are the holes (the Historical Statistics ends in 1975; Mental Health Statistics volumes exist to 1980 at least, not read).
- **Overcrowding** was normal: patients exceeded bed capacity by 11.7 per cent in 1955 and 12.2 per cent in 1956; the 1936 Year Book called the 1934 situation seriously overcrowded (36,571 resident, 2,535 more on parole, against a normal capacity of 34,866) (page, MH-5). A model of a "mental hospital building" can run above 100 per cent occupancy in the 1930s-60s.
- **Parole.** In 1964, 81.6 per cent of patients on the books were in institutions, the rest on probation (73.9 per cent of the absent), boarding out (18.7) or otherwise absent (7.4); the outside share rose every year from 1958 (page, MH-4): community care before the beds were cut.
- **Conflicts kept, not resolved.** (1) TAC 558,922 against Torrey 558,239 (US 1955). (2) Lehmann's paper July 1954 (Stip) against February 1954 (the record). (3) Largactil in France November (INHN) against December 1952 (Science History). (4) Riverview peak 4,306 / 4,630 / 4,726. (5) Snippet from a CMAJ page: 65,000 beds in 1959 and 10,653 beds "today" (the page refused, year unknown, so not used). (6) Gronfein found no break in US discharge rates when the drugs arrived, while Canada's discharges more than doubled in 1955-63 and yet the long-stay discharge rate did not move (Richman); the Canadian pattern reads as faster turnover of new admissions, not emptied wards.
- **Gaps.** 1900-1930 and 1976-2018 patient numbers; Largactil's Canadian sale date; NIMH 1964 improvement percentages; a Canadian schizophrenia employment rate; a Canadian standardised mortality ratio and years of life lost; present cost per psychiatric patient-day; 1950s psychiatrist counts; the federal prison headcount; the Hospital Insurance Act's wording on psychiatric units.
